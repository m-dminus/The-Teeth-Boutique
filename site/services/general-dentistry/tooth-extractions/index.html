General & Preventive

When letting go is the right call

We work hard to save teeth — and tell you outright when saving one no longer serves you. Then we remove it gently and plan what comes next.

Extraction is the treatment of last resort, and it's presented that way: if a tooth can reasonably be saved with a filling, crown, or root canal therapy, you'll hear those options first, with real odds. But some teeth are past the line — fractured below the gum, hollowed by decay, loosened by advanced periodontal disease, or crowding out an orthodontic result — and for those, a well-done extraction is the kindest dentistry available.

The procedure itself is calmer than its reputation. The area is thoroughly numbed and tested before anything begins; you'll feel pressure, not pain, and it's usually over sooner than patients expect. You leave with specific written aftercare — protecting the clot, managing swelling, what to eat, what to avoid — and a direct line to us if anything feels wrong.

Just as important is the next chapter: a missing tooth lets neighbors drift and bone recede, so we'll talk through replacement — implant, bridge, or partial — before you're ever in the chair, not as an afterthought.

Good to know

Questions, answered

What's dry socket, and how do I avoid it?

It's the painful loss of the healing clot from the socket in the first days after extraction. Your aftercare instructions exist largely to prevent it: no smoking, no straws or forceful spitting, gentle rinsing only when instructed. Follow them and your odds are good.

Do I have to replace an extracted tooth?

Not always — but for most visible and chewing positions, yes, replacement protects your bite and bone. We'll tell you which category your tooth is in and what each option costs.
