Emergency

Pain is information

A toothache is your tooth reporting a problem — decay, infection, a crack, a failing restoration. The report deserves a response.

Tooth pain has a vocabulary. Sensitivity to cold that fades quickly is a different message than heat sensitivity that lingers; a dull ache under chewing pressure differs from spontaneous throbbing that wakes you at night. Each pattern points somewhere — which is why describing your pain carefully when you call helps us respond well.

Until you're seen: rinse gently with warm salt water, floss to dislodge anything trapped between teeth, and use over-the-counter pain relief as directed on its label. A cold compress outside the cheek can calm throbbing. Avoid heat packs, avoid placing aspirin against the gum (it burns tissue), and avoid chewing on that side.

The uncomfortable truth: toothaches that vanish on their own didn't necessarily resolve — nerve pain that suddenly stops can mean the nerve has died, with infection still progressing silently. Pain that leaves is still a reason to be examined.

Good to know

Questions, answered

When is a toothache an ER problem instead?

Swelling affecting your eye, throat, breathing, or swallowing; fever with facial swelling; or trauma with uncontrolled bleeding — go to an emergency room. For tooth pain without those signs, your dentist is the right first call.

Will I need a root canal?

Not necessarily — toothaches have many causes and many fixes, from a filling to gum treatment. If the nerve is involved, root canal therapy is the tooth-saving option, and it's far more comfortable than its reputation.
