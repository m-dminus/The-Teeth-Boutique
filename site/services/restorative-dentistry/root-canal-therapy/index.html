Restorative

The most slandered procedure in dentistry

A root canal doesn't cause the famous pain — it ends it. The toothache is the disease; this is the cure that keeps your tooth.

Inside every tooth is a soft core of nerve and blood vessels. When deep decay or a crack lets bacteria reach it, the result is the classic severe toothache — throbbing, heat-sensitive, sleep-stealing. At that point there are two exits: remove the tooth, or remove the infection and keep the tooth. Root canal therapy is the second option, and where feasible, the better one — nothing replaces a natural tooth as well as the tooth itself.

The procedure, performed non-surgically through the crown of the tooth, removes the infected tissue, disinfects and shapes the canals, and seals them against reinfection — all under thorough local anesthesia. With modern technique and magnification, most patients compare the experience to getting a large filling, and the crushing pre-treatment pain is typically relieved from the first visit.

Because a root-canaled tooth is more brittle than a living one, back teeth are usually crowned afterward to protect the investment — a step we plan with you from the start, so there are no surprise chapters.

What to expect

How the tooth gets saved

  1. Diagnose precisely

    Testing and imaging confirm the nerve is the culprit — you'll see the evidence before treatment is proposed.

  2. Numb thoroughly, then begin

    Anesthesia is established and verified. The infected tissue is removed through a small access in the tooth.

  3. Clean, shape, seal

    The canals are disinfected and sealed against reinfection — precision work, done under magnification.

  4. Restore for the long run

    The tooth is rebuilt, usually with a crown on back teeth, and returns to full quiet service.

Good to know

Questions, answered

Honestly — does it hurt?

The procedure is done under thorough local anesthesia; what most patients actually feel is relief, because the pre-treatment toothache is the painful part. Some post-treatment tenderness for a few days is normal and manageable.

Why not just pull the tooth?

Extraction is sometimes right — but it trades one problem for several: a gap to fill, bone that recedes, neighbors that drift. Where a root canal has a good prognosis, keeping your own tooth is usually the smaller total cost, and we'll put the comparison in front of you.
